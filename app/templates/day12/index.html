Day 12 · The Tipping Point

Mild preferences,
extreme outcomes.

Each agent wants some fraction of their neighbors to be the same group. The number sounds modest. Watch what happens.

In 1971, economist Thomas Schelling proposed a thought experiment. Place two groups of people on a grid. Give each person a single preference: they want at least some percentage of their neighbors to be the same group as them. If they're unhappy, they move to a random empty spot. Then ask: what does this society look like after everyone settles?

The answer surprised him. It might surprise you too. Adjust the threshold below, click Start, and find out.

About this model

Thomas Schelling won the 2005 Nobel Prize in Economics partly for this work. The original paper was titled "Dynamic Models of Segregation" (Journal of Mathematical Sociology, 1971). What it showed: collective outcomes can emerge from individual decisions in ways that don't reflect anyone's individual preferences.

This isn't a perfect map of real-world segregation, which involves history, law, economics, and active discrimination. But it shows something genuine about how systems behave — and how easily collective patterns can form from individually defensible choices. Read more on Wikipedia →
