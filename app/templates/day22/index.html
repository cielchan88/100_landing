Day 22 · Riba & Risk

Two systems.
One shock.
Watch the mechanics.

A conventional, interest-based economy and an Islamic, profit-sharing economy face the same shock — side by side. Not to crown a winner, but to see how their mechanics differ.

This is a stylized teaching model, not a forecast and not a verdict. Both systems carry genuine trade-offs that economists still debate. Real Islamic finance operates within dual-banking systems with imperfect risk-sharing. Treat the curves as intuition about mechanisms, not predictions about any real country.

The model is a transparent discrete-time dynamic system run for 20 periods. The conventional economy uses a Taylor-style policy rule, an IS curve reacting to deviations of the real interest rate from neutral, a Phillips curve linking inflation to the output gap, and a debt path that compounds at the policy rate and grows during expansions. The Islamic economy has no policy rate; the rate of return on financing scales with the real-economy linkage, and a fixed share of each output shock is absorbed by profit-and-loss sharing instead of being pushed through to a default.

Simplifications include: closed economy (no exchange rate), no banking sector microstructure, no heterogeneity across agents or sectors, identical potential output in both economies, identical persistence parameters, and a single stylized PLS specification. The supply-side price-shock channel passes through in both systems — risk-sharing dampens losses, not the cost-push price level. Real Islamic finance operates in dual-banking systems where many products mimic debt, so the absorption parameter is best read as an upper bound.

Every one of these is a simplification a real model would treat with far more care. The model is built to show mechanism, not to forecast.
